📚 What is Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome?

Ehlers-Danlos Syndromes (EDS) are a group of 13 inherited connective tissue disorders caused by genetic mutations affecting collagen and related proteins. Connective tissue provides support and structure throughout the body, so EDS can affect the skin, joints, blood vessels, and other organs.

Genetic Condition

EDS is caused by mutations in genes that affect collagen production or structure. Most types can be confirmed through genetic testing, except hEDS.

👨‍👩‍👧

Inherited

EDS is passed from parent to child through either dominant or recessive inheritance patterns, depending on the type.

🏥

Multi-System

Because connective tissue is everywhere, EDS can affect joints, skin, blood vessels, digestive system, heart, and more.

⚠️

Often Misdiagnosed

The average patient waits 10+ years for diagnosis. Increased awareness helps patients get proper care sooner.

📋 The 13 Types of EDS (2017 Classification)

In 2017, the international classification recognized 13 distinct types of EDS. Each type has specific genetic causes and clinical features. Genetic testing can confirm diagnosis for all types except hEDS.

Hypermobile EDS (hEDS)

Most Common
Gene: Unknown (clinical diagnosis only)

Characterized by generalized joint hypermobility, chronic pain, frequent dislocations/subluxations, soft stretchy skin, fatigue, and often associated with POTS, MCAS, and GI issues. This is the only type without a known genetic marker.

📊 Affects ~1 in 3,100–5,000 people (90% of all EDS cases)

Classical EDS (cEDS)

Rare
Genes: COL5A1, COL5A2, rarely COL1A1

Very stretchy, fragile skin that bruises easily and heals with characteristic "cigarette paper" scars. Significant joint hypermobility with dislocations.

📊 Affects ~1 in 20,000–40,000 people

Vascular EDS (vEDS)

Serious
Gene: COL3A1 (rarely COL1A1)

The most serious type. Characterized by thin, translucent skin, easy bruising, and risk of spontaneous arterial/organ rupture. Requires careful medical monitoring.

📊 Affects ~1 in 50,000–200,000 people | Median life expectancy: 48 years

Kyphoscoliotic EDS (kEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Genes: PLOD1, FKBP14

Severe muscle weakness at birth, progressive scoliosis, fragile eyes prone to rupture, and joint hypermobility.

Classical-like EDS (clEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Gene: TNXB

Similar to classical EDS but without the characteristic atrophic scarring. Features skin hyperextensibility and generalized joint hypermobility.

Cardiac-valvular EDS (cvEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Gene: COL1A2

Severe progressive cardiac-valvular problems, skin involvement, and joint hypermobility. Requires cardiac monitoring.

Arthrochalasia EDS (aEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Genes: COL1A1, COL1A2

Severe joint hypermobility with congenital hip dislocation, fragile skin with easy bruising, and muscle weakness.

Dermatosparaxis EDS (dEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Gene: ADAMTS2

Extremely fragile skin that sags and wrinkles, severe bruising, and soft, doughy skin texture.

Brittle Cornea Syndrome (BCS)

Ultra-Rare
Genes: ZNF469, PRDM5

Thin corneas prone to rupture, blue sclerae, keratoconus, and hearing loss. Joint hypermobility may be present.

Spondylodysplastic EDS (spEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Genes: B4GALT7, B3GALT6, SLC39A13

Short stature, muscle weakness, bowing of limbs, and characteristic facial features.

Musculocontractural EDS (mcEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Genes: CHST14, DSE

Congenital multiple contractures, characteristic facial features, fragile skin, and internal organ anomalies.

Myopathic EDS (mEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Gene: COL12A1

Congenital muscle weakness that improves with age, joint hypermobility, and soft skin.

Periodontal EDS (pEDS)

Ultra-Rare
Genes: C1R, C1S

Severe, early-onset periodontitis leading to tooth loss, lack of attached gingiva, and pretibial plaques.

🩺 Common Signs & Symptoms

Symptoms vary significantly between types and individuals. Many people with EDS experience a combination of these symptoms. The severity can range from mild to severely disabling.

Joint & Musculoskeletal

🦴 Joint hypermobility (unusually flexible joints)
⚡ Frequent joint dislocations and subluxations
😰 Chronic joint and muscle pain
🔄 Joint instability and "clicking"
💪 Muscle weakness and fatigue
🦵 Flat feet and abnormal gait

Skin

✨ Soft, velvety, or stretchy skin
🩹 Fragile skin that tears easily
💜 Easy bruising
📍 Poor wound healing, abnormal scars
🔵 Stretch marks (striae)

Systemic & Associated Conditions

😴 Chronic fatigue
💓 POTS (Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia)
🫀 Mitral valve prolapse
🤧 MCAS (Mast Cell Activation Syndrome)
🫃 Digestive issues (IBS, gastroparesis)
😵‍💫 Dizziness and fainting
🧠 Brain fog and concentration issues
😴 Sleep disturbances

⚠️ When to Seek Emergency Care

Especially for those with or suspected of having vascular EDS:

  • Sudden severe pain in the chest, abdomen, or limbs
  • Sudden difficulty breathing
  • Signs of internal bleeding
  • Sudden severe headache
  • Unexplained swelling or bruising

🔬 How is EDS Diagnosed?

Diagnosis typically involves clinical evaluation, family history, and for most types, genetic testing. The 2017 diagnostic criteria provide a framework for diagnosis. The Ehlers-Danlos Society is working on updated criteria expected in 2026.

👨‍⚕️

Clinical Evaluation

A doctor examines joint mobility, skin elasticity, scarring patterns, and other physical signs. The Beighton score measures joint hypermobility.

👨‍👩‍👧

Family History

EDS is inherited, so family history of similar symptoms, hypermobility, or confirmed EDS diagnoses is important.

🧬

Genetic Testing

Can confirm 12 of the 13 types. Currently, there is no genetic test for hEDS—it's diagnosed clinically.

📋

2017 Criteria

International diagnostic criteria specify requirements for each type. hEDS has specific criteria including Beighton score and systemic features.

🗓️ Road to 2026

The Ehlers-Danlos Society is leading an international effort to update EDS classification and diagnostic criteria. A scientific symposium in Toronto (2025) will present findings, with final publication expected in late 2026.

⏱️ The Diagnostic Delay

On average, EDS patients wait 10 years from first symptoms to diagnosis. The median time from seeking a GP opinion to diagnosis is also years. Early diagnosis can help prevent complications and improve quality of life.
